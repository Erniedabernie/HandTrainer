Tendon glides — the five hand shapes

Your finger tendons run through the operated zone at the wrist. After 8 weeks in plaster they stick to their tunnels unless you slide them through their full travel daily. These five shapes do exactly that — each one slides a different tendon combination.

1 · Straight
all fingers long
2 · Hook
claw the fingertips
3 · Full fist
everything curls
4 · Tabletop
bend knuckles only
5 · Straight fist
fingers flat to palm
  1. Straight hand — fingers and thumb fully straight, like a salute.
  2. Hook fist — keep the big knuckles (where fingers meet palm) straight, bend the two finger joints so fingertips claw toward the top of the palm. Looks like a bear claw.
  3. Full fist — curl everything into a normal (gentle) fist, thumb outside.
  4. Tabletop — bend only the big knuckles to 90° with the fingers themselves dead straight — the hand makes an L, flat like a tabletop.
  5. Straight fist — bend the big knuckles and the middle joints but keep the end joints straight, so the flat pads of the fingers press against the palm rather than the nails.

Cycle 1→5, hold each ~2 s. That whole cycle ×10 = one set. The wrist stays still throughout — this is fingers only.

Why: keeps tendons sliding freely past the surgical scar so you don't trade a stable wrist for stiff fingers.

Thumb opposition
  1. Hand open, palm facing you.
  2. Touch the tip of the thumb to the tip of the index finger, making an O.
  3. Open fully, then thumb tip to middle fingertip. Open. Then ring. Open. Then little finger.
  4. That sweep across all four fingers = 1 repetition. Do 10.

Make a real O each time — the thumb swings across the palm, not just a lazy tap. Fully open between touches.

Why: the thumb's ability to rotate across the palm is what half of surgical instrument handling depends on, and it stiffens fast in a cast.

Thumb retropulsion — your daily tendon self-check
  1. Palm flat on a table.
  2. Lift only the thumb straight up off the table, as high as it goes. Fingers stay down.

This tests the tendon (EPL) that runs directly over where your bone tunnel was drilled. If one day the thumb won't lift when it did before — call the team the same day. Ten seconds, once a day, ideally at the same time.

Wrist bends — the four directions, and the setup that matters

The setup is most of the exercise. Forearm resting flat on a table, palm down, with the hand hanging off the edge. The table stops your elbow and shoulder helping, and palm-down switches off the one forearm muscle that pulls your carpal bones the wrong way.

  1. Extension — lift the hand up (knuckles toward ceiling) to this week's limit, 2 s up, 2 s back to level.
  2. Flexion — drop the hand down (knuckles toward floor) to the limit, same tempo.
  3. Deviations — now roll the forearm thumb-side-up (like offering a handshake), rest it on the table: tip the hand toward the thumb side (radial deviation — the tightly limited one) and toward the little-finger side (ulnar deviation).
  4. Rotation — elbow bent 90° and pinned against your ribs: turn palm up, then palm down. Full range, no limit on this one. Keeping the elbow pinned is the trick — if it drifts out, the shoulder is doing the turning.

All active — the hand moves itself; the other hand never pushes it. No bouncing at the end of range, no stretching sensation. The week's degree limits are on each day card; the app's home screen shows today's.

Dart-thrower's motion — the one that matters most
  1. Forearm supported palm-down, elbow at your side.
  2. Move the hand up-and-toward-the-thumb-side, then down-and-toward-the-little-finger-side. One smooth diagonal — exactly the motion of hammering a nail, or throwing a dart.
  3. Stay in the middle of the range: stop well before any stretch or pull at either end. Slow, continuous, no pausing at the ends.

The full picture of why this diagonal is safe (and which diagonal is dangerous) is in the Exercises tab. The short version: along this path your two key wrist bones move together, so the reconstruction between them is barely stressed. The reverse diagonal — hand toward the thumb side while bent down — is the single movement most likely to hurt the graft. Never combine those two.

If you only manage four minutes today, this is the block you keep.

Position sense drill (JPS)

Your wrist ligaments used to tell your brain where the joint was without looking. The torn ones can't, and the graft never will — so you retrain the sense using the muscles' own feedback.

  1. Set up as for wrist bends (forearm on table, hand off the edge).
  2. Eyes open: move to the target position on the day card — e.g. "15° extension" means lifted up about half of a 30° arc; use a goniometer app or just consistent landmarks.
  3. Hold it 3 s. Really register how it feels. Back to level.
  4. Close your eyes. Move to the same position from feel alone. Open — how far off were you?
  5. ×6 per target. Note your typical miss in degrees — it's a number worth logging weekly; under 5° by week 4 is the goal.
Movement detection drill (kinaesthesia)
  1. Same setup. Eyes closed, left wrist completely relaxed.
  2. Your right hand grips the left hand and moves the wrist extremely slowly — a few degrees over several seconds, in a random direction.
  3. Say "now" the instant you feel it move, and name the direction.
  4. ×5 per direction. Over the weeks, move slower — you're training the alarm to fire earlier.

Important boundary: the right hand carries the left through tiny slow movements only — it never pushes toward end of range. This is the one exception to "the other hand never touches it".

Mirror therapy
  1. Sit at a table with a mirror standing vertically in front of your chest, reflective side facing your right hand. Left hand hidden behind the mirror.
  2. Look at the reflection of your right hand — positioned so it appears exactly where your left hand would be.
  3. Move the right wrist through slow, comfortable dart-throwing arcs and finger movements while watching the reflection. Your brain reads it as the left hand moving perfectly and painlessly.
  4. 5 minutes, twice a day. A large tablet propped up with the front camera as a mirror works too.

Why: eight weeks of immobility shrinks the brain's map of the hand; this is the cheapest way to redraw it, and it has reasonable evidence for pain and function after wrist injuries.

Dexterity menu — pen rolling, coin walking, and friends

All of these share one rule: the wrist stays still in a comfortable neutral; the fingers do everything. If the wrist starts moving or working, the object is too heavy or you're rushing.

  • Pen rolling — hold a pen across the middle segments of your fingers, palm up. Use fingertips and thumb to roll it up toward the fingertips and back down. Then try rotating it end-over-end between thumb and index.
  • Coin walking — hold a coin between thumb and index knuckle-side, then pass it over the index to sit between index and middle fingers, then middle→ring, ring→little, and walk it back. Drop it constantly at first; that's the point.
  • Card turning — deck of cards face down; pick up one card at a time with the left hand and flip it face up. Speed comes with days.
  • Buttons — button and unbutton a shirt with the left hand leading.
  • Small-object transfer / 9-hole peg (from week 2) — 9 small objects (pegs, coins, dried beans), move them one at a time into a container ~20 cm away and back. Time the full cycle with your phone — same objects, same layout each time — and log it weekly. Do the same once with the right hand for your comparison target.
  • Chopstick pickup (from week 2) — chopsticks in the left hand, move soft objects (cotton balls → beans as you improve) between two bowls.
  • Fist-with-still-wrist (from day 12) — make a full fist while deliberately holding the wrist dead level, then open the fingers fully, wrist still level. Sounds trivial; after a cast the wrist wants to move with the fingers. Instrument handling needs them independent.
  • Knot tying (from week 3) — surgical silk or any string: tie one- and two-handed knots with zero pull-through tension — lay the throws down loosely. From week 4, snug them gently onto a folded towel. The ban is on pulling hard, not on tying.
Isometric holds — "push without moving" (from 01/09, only if approved on 24/08)

An isometric is a muscle contraction where nothing moves — you push against something immovable at well below full effort. It wakes the muscles that protect your reconstruction without bending the wrist.

  1. Wrist lifters: forearm palm-down on the table. Rest your right palm on the back of the left hand. Try to lift the left hand up and slightly toward the thumb side — while the right hand prevents any actual movement. Effort ~20% (feels like "barely trying").
  2. Thumb spreader: try to move the thumb out sideways away from the palm while a right-hand finger blocks it.
  3. Palm presser: palm-down on the table — press the palm gently down into the table and toward the thumb side.
  4. All together: a light "brace the wrist" clench of all of the above at once, still nothing moving.

Each: hold 5 s, rest 5 s, ×5, once daily (week 3). From 08/09: ~30% effort, ×8, twice daily. Any ache at the back of the wrist between the two forearm bones = stop, don't restart without advice.

Circumduction (from 06/09)
  1. Same table setup. Trace a slow, small circle with your hand: up → thumb-side-up-diagonal → across → little-finger-side-down-diagonal → around.
  2. Keep the circle well inside your limits in every direction — a small smooth oval, not a maximal sweep. ×5 each direction.

This is the first drill that passes through every part of your range in one movement — a graduation exercise, added late deliberately. Stop instantly on any dorsal wrist twinge.

Right-arm training days (cross-education)

Training the right side measurably slows strength loss on the immobilised left — the effect crosses over through the nervous system. Every other day, right side only, 3 sets each to comfortable fatigue:

  • Grip — squeeze a hand gripper, stress ball, or rolled towel hard, 5 s holds.
  • Wrist curls — light dumbbell or a filled water bottle, palm up, curl the wrist up and down.
  • Reverse curls — same, palm down.
  • Thumb-side raises — hold the bottle like a hammer, tip it up and down.

Right hand only. The left does nothing in these — not even holding the bottle.

Reverse wrist curls — the core exercise of this block

This is extension against weight, and it loads ECRL and ECRB — the two muscles every source agrees protect the scapholunate joint. It is the single most valuable strength exercise you have right now.

  1. Sit. Rest your forearm along your thigh or a table with the palm facing down — this is the pronated position, and it is not optional. The stabilising effect of these muscles is strongest in pronation and reverses in supination.
  2. Let the hand hang off the edge, wrist relaxed downward.
  3. Holding this week's dumbbell, lift the back of the hand upward as far as comfortable. Two seconds up.
  4. Two seconds down, under control. The lowering half matters as much as the lifting half.
  5. 2 sets of 10. Do it immediately after your passive stretch, in the range you have just gained.

If you have no dumbbell, a filled water bottle works — 500 ml is 0.5 kg, a litre is 1 kg. Convenient, and exactly the weights this block asks for.

Wrist curls — flexion, at half the weight

Same setup but palm facing up, lifting the palm toward you. Flexion is the direction that stresses the graft, so this runs at about half your reverse-curl weight throughout the block.

  1. Forearm supported, palm up, hand hanging off the edge.
  2. Curl the palm up, two seconds. Lower, two seconds.
  3. 2 sets of 10. Stop the set if anything sharp appears on the back of the wrist.
Radial deviation isometric — muscle work without the joint position

This one is worth understanding rather than just doing. Radial deviation — tilting the hand toward the thumb — is the direction that opens the scapholunate gap, and it is the direction your own follow-up documented widening in. But the muscles that drive radial deviation (ECRL, APL) are protective ones you want strong.

The resolution is to work the muscles without letting the joint travel: contract into radial deviation while the wrist stays neutral and nothing moves.

  1. Forearm resting, wrist straight and in line with the forearm — not tilted either way.
  2. Place your right hand against the thumb side of your left hand.
  3. Push the left hand gently toward the thumb side against that resistance. Nothing should move. About half effort.
  4. Hold 5 seconds, release 5 seconds. ×8.

If the wrist visibly tilts, you are pushing too hard. The whole point is the muscle contracting while the joint stays still.

Thumb band work — APL and EPB

Loads abductor pollicis longus directly, which is on the protective list, at almost no cost to the carpus. It also gives you a daily read on the thumb tendons, and your scaphoid tunnel was drilled underneath EPL.

  1. Loop a light rubber band around both thumbs, or around the thumb and index finger.
  2. Move the thumb away from the palm, out sideways, against the band. Two seconds out, two back.
  3. 2 sets of 10. Light band — this is not a strength contest.
Using the turnbuckle

A turnbuckle is a static progressive orthosis: it holds the wrist at a fixed angle and you screw it a little further as the tissue relaxes. It works on total time at end range, not on force — which is why it is worn for half an hour rather than cranked hard for two minutes.

  1. Fit it and adjust to a comfortable stretch. Comfortable, not painful — pain makes tissue guard and defeats it.
  2. Hold about 5 minutes. If it has eased, adjust a little further.
  3. Repeat through a 30 minute block. Up to 3 blocks a day.
  4. The step people skip: when you take it off, immediately do 10 active holds at the new range. Five seconds each, no assistance. Otherwise you have widened the range without learning to use it.

Extension blocks daily. Flexion blocks from 23/09, shorter to start. Never both at maximum in the same session, and never a flexion block straight after a loading session.

The rotation wheel — one thing to change

The pronation/supination wheel is fine as a range-of-motion tool and worth keeping. One modification if it carries any resistance:

  • Load the pronation half — turning the palm downward — and let the supination half return assisted or unweighted.
  • Resisted supination destabilises the scapholunate joint in cadaveric work, and separately it loads a TFCC foveal tear and dorsally subluxed ulnar head that were never formally addressed.
  • Unloaded rolling in both directions is fine and always has been. It is specifically resistance into supination that is the problem.
Sustained neutral hold — the one that fails first in theatre

Nobody trains static endurance and it is the thing that gives out during a long case. Strength is not the same as the ability to hold a position for twenty minutes.

  1. Stand as if scrubbed — elbows flexed, forearms up, hands in front of you, unsupported.
  2. Wrists level, in line with the forearms.
  3. Hold 2 minutes. Rest 1 minute. ×3. Build to 3 minutes each from 03/10.

Why 2 kg, when you were cleared for 5

  • The 5 kg is a general lifting ceiling — carrying, holding, a bag. Use all of it, with a straight wrist.
  • A dumbbell in a wrist curl acts on a lever about 9 cm long. 2 kg on a curl bends the wrist more than carrying 5 kg with it straight, where the load hangs through the joint and barely bends it at all.
  • The kilogram figure is about what is in your hand. The risk is about where your wrist is.

Which pulls, in which forearm position

A generic "wrist strengthening" programme loads the SL-protective and SL-adverse muscles equally. They are not interchangeable.

Build these

  • Reverse wrist curls (extension) — ECRL, ECRB — forearm pronated. The core exercise of the block; these are the agreed protective extensors and they stabilise best in pronation.
  • Radial deviation isometrics, wrist held neutral — ECRL, APL. Gets the radial stabilisers working without taking the joint into radial deviation, which is the gapping position.
  • Thumb abduction/extension against a light band — APL, EPB. Loads APL at almost no carpal cost, and keeps an eye on EPL.
  • Extension holds at your new end range — step 3 of the loop, doubling as strength work.

Keep moderate

  • Wrist flexion curls — about half your extension weight. Flexion is the direction that stresses the graft, and the flexors are not the muscles protecting it.
  • Grip — from 25/09 only. Submaximal, short bouts. A clenched fist is the stress view used to show an SL gap on X-ray; grip is a provocation, not a neutral exercise.
Defer or avoid.
  • Resisted supination — destabilises the SL joint in cadaveric work, and loads a TFCC foveal tear and dorsally subluxed ulnar head that were never addressed. On the rotation wheel, load the pronation half and let supination return assisted.
  • Resisted ulnar deviation — ECU is the one muscle every source agrees is bad for the SL.
  • Anything loaded through an extended wrist — press-ups, palm planks, burpees, leaning on the heel of the hand. Not a weight question.
  • Maximal grip testing beyond a single dynamometer reading for the record.
Honest caveat on two muscles. Sources contradict each other on FCR and FCU — one teaching source calls FCU protective and FCR problematic in later phases; the cadaveric work argues close to the opposite. What they agree on is ECU adverse, ECRL/ECRB/APL protective. This block is built on the agreed part.

Turnbuckle

Dosing, once it arrives

  • 30 minutes per direction, re-adjusting to a new comfortable stretch roughly every 5 minutes within the block.
  • Up to 3 blocks a day. Total time at end range is what drives the gain, not the force.
  • Passive time — do it reading, at a handover, watching something.

Sequencing — yours is adjustable both ways

  • Extension first, and daily. It is the functional priority and the safer direction for the graft.
  • Flexion second, from 23/09, shorter and fewer to start.
  • Never both at maximum in the same session, and never a flexion block straight after a loading session.
  • Always follow a block with step 3 — active holds at the new range. A turnbuckle without active follow-up just widens the gap.

The splint is not for your exercises — it is for the unguarded moment: a stumble, a knock in a corridor, rolling onto it asleep. A hospital at handover is a riskier place for your wrist than your living room. The OT's prescription on 24/08 replaces this schedule.

Swelling — you have none, which is good news. Keep one habit:

  • Weekly figure-8 tape measurement, both wrists (tape from the wrist crease diagonally around the hand and back — same landmarks each time; log it in Numbers).
  • Why bother when there's no swelling? Because new swelling appearing at week 3–4 — especially with pain out of proportion or colour/temperature change — is an early warning of a real problem (see surveillance below), and you only catch "new" if you have a baseline trend.
  • Everything else from the standard oedema playbook — elevation routines, compression, retrograde massage — you can skip unless puffiness actually appears after heavy days. If it does: elevate + massage that evening and tell the therapist at the next visit.

Scars & the two nerve patches

  • Sites: the long scar on the back of the wrist, the small one on the front wrist crease (where the graft tendon was taken), and the old keyhole/pin dots.
  • Silicone gel 12 h/day on all of them for ~3 months.
  • Massage: firm little circles, 2 min, twice daily — around the scars this week, directly over them from next week. A mobile scar stays comfortable; a stuck one tethers the tendons underneath it.
  • Desensitisation: two patches of skin had their small nerves disturbed — the back of the hand near the thumb, and the heel of the palm. Rub each with three textures (cotton → towel → velcro), 60 s each, twice daily. Weird tingling that settles = normal and improving; spreading numbness = report it.
  • Post-cast skin: moisturise daily, no scrubbing, no long hot soaks.

Fuel the graft

  • Protein ≥1.6 g/kg/day, spread over the day — the graft is remodelling collagen for months.
  • No calorie deficit until at least the 06/10 review. Not the window to cut.
  • Sleep 7–9 h — tissue repair is mostly nocturnal. Splint on overnight.
  • Alcohol minimal — it measurably impairs tendon-to-bone healing. No nicotine in any form — the single worst legal molecule for a healing graft.
  • Vitamin C 500 mg/day: cheap, near-harmless, evidence honestly mixed. Your call.

Work, driving, gym

  • Carrying and lifting: up to 5 kg with a straight wrist, load close to the body. This is what the surgeon gave you on 08/09 and you can use all of it.
  • Gym: legs, core, right arm, cardio at full intensity. Pulling — rows, pull-ups, deadlifts — is fine with a neutral wrist; use straps to cut the grip demand. Nothing loaded through an extended wrist (see the card below).
  • Theatre: scrubbing and left-hand instruments still need surgeon sign-off. Assisting is worse than operating — sustained retraction is the highest static load your wrist meets. Ask for written return-to-theatre criteria on 06/10.
  • Driving: confirm with the surgeon. A swerve or an airbag is a real load.
  • At the desk and the table: stop leaning on the heel of your hand. That is loaded wrist extension held for minutes, several times a day — the highest-volume version of the position you are avoiding in the gym.

Weekly self-surveillance — 2 minutes, same day each week

  • Thumb lift off the table (daily, not weekly — it's ten seconds).
  • Figure-8 both wrists → log it.
  • Press once on the back of the wrist just beyond the bony bump at the end of the forearm (Lister's tubercle): new tenderness there that wasn't there last week = tell the team this week.
  • Compare hands: colour, temperature, sweatiness, hair. Two or more clearly different + pain out of proportion → call in days, not weeks — early treatment of that syndrome (CRPS) is the whole game.
  • Stop-today flags: a pop/snap with pain · a new bump on the back of the wrist · pain now waking you at night · a movement you had yesterday gone today · thumb won't lift.
Hard rules until 17/09
  • No gripping against resistance — no putty, gripper, or dynamometer (except a sanctioned baseline).
  • No weight-bearing through the hand; no pushing up out of chairs with it.
  • No lifting beyond a coffee cup (~0.5 kg); no carrying; no bags hooked on the fingers.
  • No passive stretching, no pushing to end of range, nobody else moving your wrist.
  • Never thumb-side tilt combined with bending down (radial deviation + flexion).
  • No impact; no sport; no operating or retraction.
  • No Watson test, no stress or clenched-fist X-ray views this phase — decline them if offered.
  • Pain ceiling 2/10 during, settled within 30 min, no worse next morning — any failure: drop back one week's parameters for 3 days.

Loading the wrist, long term

Nobody on your team raised this and you were surprised to have any. Worth putting to the surgeon on 06/10 so it comes from him.

  • Honest strength of evidence: weak. The 2022 systematic review of long-term outcomes after SL reconstruction found only six studies past five years, all level IV, and no evidence-based activity recommendations. These are precautions from mechanism, not rules from a trial.
  • The mechanism is sound though. A tendon graft is not a ligament and creeps differently. Loading through an extended wrist drives the capitate into the SL interval — the exact force the graft resists. Gap recurrence over time is common in the published series. And your construct has no metal backup.
  • The one rule: load through a neutral wrist is fine, load through an extended wrist is the problem. Weight is secondary.

Substitutions worth making permanent

  • Press-ups on flat palms → fists, parallettes or dumbbell handles
  • Palm planks → forearm planks
  • Burpees → step-back onto handles, or row / bike / sled for the conditioning
  • Bench with the wrist laid back → bar stacked over a neutral wrist, plus wraps
  • Straight-bar overhead press → neutral-grip dumbbell or landmine
  • Front squat clean rack → straps, cross-arm, or a safety squat bar
  • Downward dog, chaturanga, crow, handstands → dolphin, forearm variants, fists, or a wedge block
  • Falls are the dominant risk — the original injury was a fall on an outstretched hand. Wrist guards for anything on wheels or ice.
Heat, and your two nerve patches. Superficial radial branches from the Op 1 pinning, and the palmar cutaneous branch of the median from the palmaris harvest. If sensation is reduced anywhere, do not put a heat pack on the numb area — that is how thermal burns happen in exactly this group. Warm water is safer: you can test it with the other hand and it cannot exceed its own temperature. Better still, warm up by moving — five minutes of glides and easy rotation warms the tissue and does the work at the same time.

Your numbers

Log whenever you measure — any day, any subset of fields. The suggested rhythm is weekly, but the date is yours.

How to measure each number — diagrams

General rules first: measure at the same time of day (angles are stiffer in the morning) · three attempts, record the best · measure the right hand too each time — it is your personal reference · consistency of method beats accuracy; measured the same way every week, even a rough method gives a perfect trend.

Wrist up / down — extension & flexion (°)

  1. Forearm flat on the table, palm down, hand over the edge — the exercise position.
  2. Lift the hand as far as comfortable. The angle between the line of the forearm (0°) and the line of the back of the hand = extension.
  3. Drop the hand down: same angle below the line = flexion.
  4. Easiest solo method: phone propped side-on at table height → photo at maximum lift → measure the angle on the screenshot with any free angle/protractor app (or iPhone Photos markup + eye). Repeatable and hands-free.

Side tilts — radial & ulnar deviation (°)

  1. Forearm and palm flat on the table. Photograph or view from directly above.
  2. Reference line: down the middle of the forearm, extended through the middle finger — that is 0°.
  3. Slide the hand toward the thumb side without lifting it: the angle the middle finger makes with the reference line = radial deviation. Toward the little-finger side = ulnar deviation.
  4. Same photo trick, taken from above.

Figure-8 (cm) — the swelling tape measurement

Use a soft sewing tape, back of the hand facing you, fingers up. Numbers below match the diagram.

  1. Start at the bony bump on the little-finger side of the wrist (dot on the diagram).
  2. Wrap across the front of the wrist to the thumb-side wrist bump (dashed — hidden behind).
  3. Run diagonally across the back of the hand up to the little-finger knuckle.
  4. Across the palm side of the knuckles (dashed) to the index knuckle.
  5. Diagonally back down across the back of the hand to the start — the two diagonals cross in an X. Read the tape where it meets the start. Same landmarks every time, tape snug but not compressing. Both wrists.

The other three

  • JPS error (°): run the position-sense drill (Moves tab) — the number to log is your average miss in degrees across the 6 eyes-closed attempts. Estimate off the same photo method if unsure.
  • 9-hole peg (s): 9 small identical objects, container ~20 cm away. Time moving all 9 in and all 9 back, left hand. Same objects, same spacing, every time. Do the right hand once a week for your target.
  • Pain end-range (0–10): the worst pain felt at the very end of your permitted range during today's sessions. 0 = nothing, 10 = worst imaginable. Trend matters: it should fall week on week.
